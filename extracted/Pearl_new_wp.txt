                                               Abstract
    This whitepaper specifies the proof-of-useful-work protocol of the Pearl blockchain, in which the unit
of mining work is FP8 matrix multiplication on GPUs, extending the integer matrix-multiplication proof
of useful work of Komargodski and Weinstein [4]. At a high level, the main idea is that post-noise
(non-linear) quantization removes the low-rank shortcut, so tickets can hash output tiles directly. For
verification purposes, the verifier recovers the bit-exact computation using the technique of Badash et
al. [5]. Security rests on the hardness of multiplying quantized, low-rank-noised matrices faster than
generically. An extension of the protocol to mixture-of-experts multiplication is also described.

1     Introduction
Proof-of-work consensus makes block production costly by design [1, 2], but classical implementations require
repeated hashings that have no value outside the protocol. A proof of useful work (PoUW) replaces the
artificial puzzle with a computation someone already wants performed [3]. The obstacle is permissionlessness:
the miner chooses its own inputs, so the protocol must guarantee that no choice of inputs, however degenerate,
makes valid tickets cheaper than the prescribed amount of work.
    This whitepaper specifies a proof-of-useful-work protocol whose unit of work is FP8 matrix multiplication
on GPUs. A miner multiplies matrices of its own choosing, for instance the operands of an AI training or
inference workload, and every tile of the product doubles as a lottery ticket for block production. The protocol
covers ordinary matrix multiplication. An optional extension covers mixture-of-experts multiplications, in
which routing is conceptually part of the left operand (Appendix A). It is the consensus layer of the Pearl
blockchain: the same multiplications that serve AI workloads secure the network.
    The starting point of this document is the PoUW of Komargodski and Weinstein [4] for arbitrary integer
matrix multiplication, whose blockchain this protocol instantiates. Their protocol perturbs the operands
with low-rank noise derived from a public seed, computes the noised product, and applies a random oracle
to the transcript of the computation. The low-rank structure lets an honest party remove the noise from
the output at negligible cost, giving a useful protocol with 1 + o(1) multiplicative overhead. The analysis
is set over exact arithmetic in the random-oracle model, where, under their direct-product conjecture, even
the all-zeroes matrices are as hard to multiply as any other pair. This document describes an alternative to
the protocol of [4] that supports floating-point matrix multiplications. Verification requires the verifier to
recompute a selected output tile bit-for-bit. This is made possible because the arithmetic of the whitelisted
GPUs is deterministic and reproducible via the technique of Badash et al. [5].

Organization. Section 2 presents the abstract protocol (low-rank noising plus quantization) and the core
hardness assumption in its cleanest setting. Section 3 fixes notation, hashing, and the work model. Section 5
specifies the jackpot policy. Section 4 specifies the plaintext verifier, Section 6 the honest useful miner,
and Section 7 the known classes of mining speedups. The appendices specify the mixture-of-experts and
zero-knowledge verifiers and the bit-exact arithmetic, sampling, and quantization procedures.

Although NA has rank r, its entrywise-quantized image Q(NA ) can have much higher rank. The central
hardness premise is that this destroys the exploitable structure: the resulting product cannot be computed
accurately with substantially less work than a generic quantized product. This premise depends on the data
type and quantizer, so the protocol whitelists both.

Assumption 1 (Informal quantized-subspace hardness). For every admissible k, there is an r ≪ k with the
following property. With high probability over random r-dimensional subspaces UA , UB ⊂ Rk , the following
holds. Draw the rows of NA , NB randomly from UA , UB , respectively. On average, computing

                                                Q(NA ) Q(NB )T

is not substantially easier than computing
                                                  Q(A) Q(B)T ,
where A, B have generic random rows in Rk . The same protocol-approved Q is used in both products. Both
computations require work comparable to roughly mnk multiply-accumulate operations.
   Concretely, the protocol uses r = 32 for k ≤ 216 . The assumption compares computational difficulty, not
the two input distributions.

Relation to the transcript-unpredictability assumption. Assumption 1 is the floating-point ana-
logue of the transcript-unpredictability assumption of [4]. There, low-rank noise makes the output of an
adversarially chosen product cheap (for A = B = 0 the noised product is just NA NBT ), so hardness must
rest on the transcript of the computation. Here, quantization blocks the output shortcut, and hardness can
rest on the output tiles themselves. A miner controls A and B and can try to plant structure that survives
noising and quantization. Section 5 specifies the policy checks that rule out such structure.

Blockchain state and the state window. A proof of work must be bound to the blockchain state, so
that work cannot be prepared before the state is known or reused across blocks. In the basic setting the
miner binds its commitments to the block header it is proposing, and the proposed header in turn commits

    We index headers by depth relative to the proposed block. Write σ0 = σ  b for the proposed header, σ1 for

    Consensus relaxes the basic setting by a state window of depth D (e.g., D = 3), allowing the miner to
bind its commitment to B to any one of the D headers σ0 , . . . , σD−1 in the window. The commitment to A
remains bound to σ  b.

Domain-separated hashes. Let q be a 32-byte parent key and let ℓ be a domain-separation string, e.g.
“jackpot”. For readability, each label displayed in this paper omits an implicit protocol-version component.
The concrete label includes both the protocol version and the displayed role. BLAKE3 accepts one key, so
q occupies its key slot. Derive the key for role ℓ by hashing the string under q:

                                     Subkey(q, ℓ) := BLAKE3(ℓ; key = q).

Then
                                 Hℓ (x; q) := BLAKE3(x; key = Subkey(q, ℓ)).
When no transcript-derived parent key is available, use unkeyed BLAKE3(ℓ) rather than Subkey. That is,

                                  Hℓ (x) := BLAKE3(x; key = BLAKE3(ℓ)).

This preserves the domain-separation interface, without key unpredictability.

Work model.      We measure work in multiply-accumulate operations (MACs). The direct cost of the selected
tile is
                                            Ttile := |IA | · |IB | · k.
We count MACs for concreteness only. Any model of computation serves, with Ttile read as the corresponding
tile cost in that model. The MAC count only fixes the scale. It intentionally excludes the work required for
quantization, noise generation, hashing, commitment verification, and memory movement.


4      Plaintext verifier
4.1     Verifier overview
The verifier checks one tile of the matmul, never the whole product. The proof names that tile and carries
only the data the tile reads. Appendix A gives the optional MoE verifier, in which routing is conceptually
part of A. Appendix B gives the zero-knowledge verifier for either mode.

    1. Receive the strips. The proof carries the selected rows of A and B. The selected B rows become
       columns of the output tile.
    2. Check the trees, derive the seeds. The strips arrive with minimal Merkle multi-proofs. The
       verifier recomputes the roots of A and B from the strips and their proofs, and rejects a mismatch. It
       derives noise seedB from the root of B and the chain state, then noise seedA from noise seedB
       and the root of A. The seeds follow the commitments, so the miner cannot pick its noise.
    3. Noise the strips, compute the tile. The seeds expand into the noise of the two strips, giving A    e

       and B. The verifier computes C = A(B) with the kernel the miner ran, so both tiles agree bit for bit.

4.2    Public parameters
The dimensions m, n, k are public. The proof also supplies a noise rank r, a Quant, a Device, operand hash
identifiers hash idA , hash idB , and miner-chosen periodic partitions PA , PB . Each hashing identifier selects
the precise way hashing is done, and is chosen from a consensus-allowed list. Notably, it controls the chunk
size internal to BLAKE3. Each partition divides an operand’s rows into strips, thus breaking the entire A · B
matmul deterministically into many independent matmuls each consisting of a fixed number of rows of A
and a fixed number of rows of B. A partition is periodic: it is described by a short pattern that repeats
along the axis, so every strip has the same shape and the strips never overlap. The pattern is encoded in a
few bytes, and a base is valid only if it lies on the pattern’s grid.
    Device and Quant are enumerations, each chosen from a consensus-allowed list. Device names the GPU
architecture, and Quant names the quantization scheme. This document specifies one such quantization
scheme: the FP10 commit type of Appendix C.6 together with the noisy quantization of Appendix C.4.
Finally, e is the number of experts of the mixture-of-experts extension (Appendix A). Ordinary matrix
multiplication sets e := 0.
    The public parameter tuples for the two operands are

                                pB := (n, k, r, Quant, Device, hash idB , PB , e),
                                                                                                               (2)
                                pA := (m, hash idA , PA ).

4.6    Verifier rationale
Openings. The verifier interprets each root under one operand shape, hash id, and key. A minimal
Merkle proof has one expected set of leaves and sibling hashes. This rule prevents proof padding and bounds
the input before arithmetic starts. An opening authenticates only the disclosed rows. It does not prove
knowledge of the undisclosed matrix entries.
    Opened values use the encoding selected by Quant. Rejecting NaN and infinity removes exceptional
arithmetic inputs.

State window. The B commitment can use any header σd in the state window. Relative to using only
σ0 , this gives at most a D-fold opportunity to preprocess the B side. For a useful miner, the same window
gives up to D − 1 block intervals to replace its matrices after the blockchain tip changes. It need not replace
them immediately. The A commitment uses and binds the proposed header.

Challenge order. The seed chain fixes the intended Fiat–Shamir order. The miner fixes B and pB before
it obtains noise seedB . It can then choose A, but it must fix A and pA before it obtains noise seedA .
This order allows A to depend on the first challenge without allowing it to depend on the second challenge.
    Including pX in each seed hash binds the operand dimensions, noise rank, Quant, Device, hash identifiers,
and partition to that noise challenge. These bindings do not prevent transcript grinding or shared work
across commitments and depths. The batched and grinding security definitions must account for those
choices.


                                © 2026 Pearl Research Labs. All rights reserved.                             8
Hash domains. The labels “key-A”, “key-B”, “seed-A”, and “seed-B” separate operand roles and tran-
script stages. The sampler uses a seed-derived key and the typed address (X, Z, ℓ) as its message. This
design gives deterministic random access and makes draw order irrelevant. The seed already binds k and r,
so the address does not repeat them.
    The “noise-line” and “jackpot” labels derive different keys from the same seed.

Arithmetic and extraction. Quant fixes the commit type, the quantized type, the quantization algo-
rithm, and roundBF16→BF16 . Device fixes the remaining arithmetic. This distinction is necessary because
E4M3FN operands alone do not determine a bit-exact result on all devices. Reproducing a GPU matmul
exactly requires pinning down rounding direction, subnormal handling, and accumulation order. Badash,
Boneh, Komargodski, and Srivastava [5] characterize these behaviors across NVIDIA architectures and pre-
cisions, including FP8, and reproduce them off the GPU. The protocol uses the specified floating-point
operations, not ideal real-number arithmetic. A non-finite intermediate result therefore causes verification
failure.

Difficulty and eligibility. Scaling the target by |IA | · |IB | · k makes the expected number of wins pro-
portional to the direct MAC cost of the checked tile. The upper tile bound limits verifier work. The lower
bounds ensure that Extract is fed with sufficient entropy.
   The policy in Section 5.1 and the target have different purposes. The policy determines tile eligibility.
The target sets the win rate for eligible tiles.


5     Jackpot policy
Beyond the idealized setting of Section 2, the protocol must stay secure when the matrices A, B are not
zero and the arithmetic is a concrete floating-point format. This opens an attack surface, which the verifier
addresses by a pointwise set of checks that either accept or reject a single lottery tile. We call it the jackpot
policy.

5.2     Attack vectors
The protocol as instantiated in Sections 5 and 4 carries a considerable amount of detail, mainly because the
permissionless setting leaves the miner many directions in which to attack. The paragraphs below describe
each direction and the mechanism that answers it.

Degenerate arithmetic. On NaN or infinite values, device arithmetic stops resembling arithmetic over
R: a NaN propagates through every later operation, and an infinity absorbs every further finite summand. A
tile driven into a non-finite state therefore collapses to a value predictable from few of its inputs. The verifier
forbids NaN and infinity in the input operands and in every intermediate result, which keeps accepted tiles
inside the regime that the checks of Section 5.1 model.

Noise seed grinding. The seeds are unpredictable, but a miner can grind them, hunting for structure in
the noise factors that admits a faster multiplication. For this reason, structure in FA , EB , or FB that yields
a meaningful speedup must be rare enough that the expected cost of grinding for it is impractical. The lower
bound k ≥ 1024 and |IB | ≥ 16 serve this requirement. Structure in EA tolerates a looser threshold, as EA
is derived from both A and B’s commitments while FA , EB , or FB are derived from B’s commitment alone.
This asymmetry is why some checks treat the two sides differently.
    For example, if grinding makes two rows of EB in the same IB identical, the jackpot for IA × IB may
cost k|IA |(|IB | − 1) rather than k|IA ||IB |. Nevertheless, the multiplicative speedup, |IB |/(|IB | − 1) < 1.1, is
acceptable given the nontrivial grinding required: SampleLine has about 150 bits of min-entropy. Similarly,
for B = 1, quantization leaves Q(B + EB FBT )ij = Bij in roughly 10% of entries. With k ≥ 1024, grinding
for a substantially larger fraction across a whole tile is impractical.

No-noise entries. A miner can commit operands whose entries dwarf the per-row noise scale. Quan-
tization then absorbs most of the noise: such an entry’s quantized value ranges over only a few adjacent
FP8 codes, so large parts of Ae and B,e and of every tile they produce, are predictable before the seeds are
known. The entry-liveness check bounds the fraction of these entries on each side. Note that the proportion
of noise-agnostic entries can be upper-bounded a priori as a function of δ alone, with no check at all, but
the resulting estimates are generally looser.

Low local entropy. A miner can commit rows whose peak entry dwarfs their typical entry. The row
normalization of Appendix C.4 must leave room for the peak, which compresses the rest of the row, noise
included, toward zero, until the quantized noise takes only a handful of small FP8 values. Operands with so
little local entropy invite specialized hardware: a circuit built for a reduced alphabet of values can multiply
faster than a general FP8 kernel, breaking the premise that a multiply-accumulate costs what the work
model charges for it. The noise-floor check removes this option by requiring every opened row to carry noise
of scale at least σmin in quantized units.

6     Useful miner overview
This section describes the intended workflow of an honest miner: one that mines with multiplications it
wants computed anyway. The miner holds a queue of jobs, each a pair A, B of R matrices whose useful
product is C = AB T , and interleaves the following steps with ordinary workload.

    1. Prequantize. Encode A, B in the commitment format (FP10). The protocol does not mandate the
       encoding procedure. A recommended construction appears in Appendix C.6.
    2. Commit. Hash B under hash idB keyed by a recent blockchain header, and derive noise seedB .
       Then commit A under the proposed header and derive noise seedA .
    3. Noise and quantize. Derive EX , FX from the seeds and compute X e = NoisyQuantizep (X, EX , FX )
                                                                                         X
       for X ∈ {A, B}. This costs O((m + n)kr) MACs and one pass over the operands.
    4. Multiply. Run MatMulDevice once on the full noised operands. This is the dominant cost, mnk MACs,
       and it is the same kernel the job would run without the protocol, applied to perturbed operands.
    5. Scan for winners. Cut the output into the tiles induced by PA , PB . For each tile compute the
       extractor output and the jackpot hash, and compare against the scaled target. Each tile wins with
       probability proportional to its cost Ttile , so the expected number of wins per job is proportional to its
       total MAC count. Losing tiles are discarded and reveal nothing.

    6. Open on a win. For a winning tile, authorized by the jackpot policy, assemble the proof of Section 4.4.




                                 © 2026 Pearl Research Labs. All rights reserved.                             12
Recovering the useful product. The above description computes C          e = AeBe T , not C. The two differ
by terms the miner can reconstruct cheaply once the seeds are known: the signal–noise cross terms carry a
rank-r factor and cost O((mn + mk + nk)r) MACs, and the per-row descaling induced by the quantization.
What cannot be removed, namely the terms involving the quantization errors (15), is of the same order as
the error that directly quantizing A and B to FP8 would introduce. The miner therefore recovers the useful
product at FP8-level accuracy for the cost of the noised product plus lower-order corrections.

Overhead accounting. Beyond the product itself, the miner pays for hashing the operands, noising,
scanning for winning tiles, and, rarely, assembling a proof. Each of these is O((mn + mk + nk)r) MACs or
a single hashing pass over A, B or C,
                                    e against mnk for the product, so the total overhead is a 1 + o(1) factor
when m, n, k ≫ r. When B is fixed in advance, its hashing and noising need only be refreshed every few
blockchain-tip changes. This preprocessing improves the concrete efficiency.


7    Known mining speedups
If we drop the usability requirement, a miner may gain a speedup relative to a useful miner. The purpose
of this section is not to specify the guardrails that mitigate these speedups, but to record which high-level
speedup classes were in scope when the protocol was designed.

Input crafting. A miner that does not need the product can choose operands for speed rather than for
use: low-rank matrices, rows dominated by a few peaks, rows whose noise quantizes away, or operand pairs
with strongly correlated rows. The jackpot policy of Section 5.1 is the defense, imposing statistical properties
that ensure unpredictability.

Precision shortcuts. The device arithmetic of Appendix C.2 truncates summands below the accumulation
grid, so a miner can skip any summand it knows will round away and save its MACs. The unpredictable-
summands check in Section 5.1 caps the fraction of skippable summands at εpred , and the tamed-products
check keeps cells out of the magnitude regime where whole groups of summands round away at once.

Seed and commitment grinding. A miner can retry commitments to search for favorable noise: ex-
ploitable structure in the factors (Section 5.2) or otherwise favorable transcripts. Each discarded attempt
costs a commitment and a seed derivation, so this class is bounded by the usual Fiat–Shamir grinding
argument.

Work reuse. A miner can try to extract several tickets from one multiplication: chaining instances by
resubmitting noised outputs as fresh inputs, or amortizing shared operands, noise, or preprocessing across
a batch of jobs. These attacks succeed only if revealing a noised tile makes the clean tile’s ticket cheaper to
predict.

Better kernels and hardware. A miner with a faster bit-exact implementation of MatMulDevice , through
better scheduling or better silicon, outpaces other miners, but this is the same advantage an honest miner
gains and is not an attack on the protocol: the work model charges everyone in the same units.

C.6    Commit type (FP10)
An operand with n rows is committed as two matrices, with k a multiple of 8:

              V ∈ {−128, . . . , 127}n×k    (integer values),            Σ ∈ BF16n×k/8     (block scales).

Each block of 8 consecutive entries of a row shares one scale, and entry (t, u) represents the real number

                                                      Vtu · Σt,⌊u/8⌋ .

The format spends 8 bits per entry plus 16 bits per block of 8, i.e. 10 bits per entry, hence the name.
   This appendix defines the decoding

                                           X := Open(V, Σ) ∈ BF16n×k

whose opened rows Appendix C.4 takes as input, and the row norms ℓ2 (Xt ) and ℓ∞ (Xt ) it uses.

Commitment. Each matrix is committed separately as a Merkle tree over its rows, under the key and
hash identifier of Section 4. A row of V is its k bytes, each value in two’s complement. A row of Σ is its 2k/8
bytes, each scale as a little-endian BF16 code. Scales that are NaN or infinite are rejected. The aggregate
root HX is a hash of the two roots RV , RΣ together with the shapes of their matrices.
